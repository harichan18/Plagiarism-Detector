NoizOff: An IoT-Based Noise Detection and Automated Complaint Generation System

Anita Joshi, Hritikha Shetty, Kunal Honde, Ayush Ingale, Harichan Kushwaha

Department of Engineering, Sciences and Humanities (DESH)

Vishwakarma Institute of Technology, Pune, Maharashtra, India

Abstract: Noise pollution has emerged as a serious environmental problem in urban regions, affecting human health and overall quality of life. Most existing solutions focus only on monitoring and visualization of noise levels, lacking an effective action-oriented response mechanism. This paper presents NoizOff, an Internet of Things (IoT) based system that detects environmental sound, computes sound intensity in decibels, compares it with the standards prescribed by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), and automatically triggers alerts and complaint notifications through email when excessive noise is detected. The proposed system integrates an INMP441 digital microphone sensor with an ESP32 microcontroller, enabling real-time data acquisition and wireless communication via Wi-Fi. The system architecture ensures continuous monitoring, accurate noise evaluation through proper calibration, and automated escalation to registered authorities and NGOs. This approach bridges the gap between noise monitoring and regulatory enforcement, providing a practical solution for urban noise management.

Keywords –  Noise Pollution, IoT, ESP32, Sound Detection, Smart City, Environmental Monitoring, Automated Complaint System

INTRODUCTION

Noise pollution is one of the most persistent and underestimated forms of environmental pollution in modern urban environments. Rapid industrialization, urban expansion, vehicular traffic growth, construction activities, and increasing population density have significantly elevated ambient noise levels across cities. Prolonged exposure to high-intensity noise has been scientifically linked to severe health complications including hearing impairment, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, sleep disorders, stress-related illnesses, and reduced cognitive performance. Despite strict regulatory frameworks and growing awareness, noise pollution remains poorly controlled due to inadequate real-time monitoring and delayed enforcement mechanisms.

Traditional noise monitoring systems primarily focus on periodic measurement and data visualization, offering little support for immediate corrective actions. In most cases, violations of permissible noise limits go unreported or are addressed only after considerable delay, which reduces the overall effectiveness of existing environmental policies. Furthermore, manual reporting mechanisms rely heavily on citizen initiative and often lack proper documentation, resulting in weak accountability and inefficient regulatory action. This highlights the urgent need for intelligent systems capable of transforming environmental sensing into actionable governance.

The NoizOff system is proposed as a comprehensive IoT-based framework designed to bridge this critical gap. By integrating real-time noise detection, regulatory threshold validation based on CPCB standards, instantaneous alert generation, and automated complaint escalation, NoizOff provides an efficient, scalable, and socially impactful solution for urban noise pollution management. The system empowers communities and authorities to respond promptly to noise violations, thereby strengthening environmental compliance, improving public health outcomes, and contributing to more sustainable and livable smart cities.

LITERATURE REVIEW

Over the past decade, significant research efforts have been directed toward the development of noise monitoring systems using Internet of Things (IoT) technology, primarily to address the growing concern of urban noise pollution. Early studies focused on the design of low-cost acoustic sensor networks capable of collecting continuous environmental noise data across wide geographical regions. These systems enabled the creation of dynamic noise maps that assist city planners in understanding noise distribution patterns and identifying high-risk zones. However, such solutions were largely limited to data acquisition and visualization without incorporating any real-time enforcement or response mechanism.

Recent advancements have introduced machine learning techniques for urban sound classification, where extracted audio features such as Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) are used with classifiers including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) to distinguish between various noise sources such as traffic, construction, industrial machinery, and human activities. While these methods improve the accuracy of noise source identification, they still function primarily as analytical tools and do not actively contribute to regulatory intervention.

Several studies have also explored crowdsensing approaches, where distributed sensors and mobile devices are used to gather noise data from multiple locations simultaneously. These platforms improve spatial coverage and provide detailed noise mapping for smart cities. Although crowdsensing enhances data availability and awareness, the collected information typically remains within monitoring dashboards and does not directly trigger any automated corrective or legal action.

A notable limitation across most existing research is the absence of a closed-loop enforcement framework. While current systems effectively detect and classify noise, they rarely incorporate automated escalation mechanisms that translate environmental violations into actionable governance. Regulatory compliance therefore remains dependent on manual reporting, administrative delays, and subjective judgment.

The proposed NoizOff system addresses this critical research gap by integrating continuous noise sensing with real-time threshold evaluation based on CPCB standards, instant alert generation, and automated complaint communication to responsible authorities. By converting noise detection into immediate regulatory action, NoizOff extends beyond traditional monitoring systems and establishes a practical model for technology-driven environmental governance.

Design

The hardware design philosophy focused on creating a "standalone edge node." This means the device performs all calculations locally on the chip, rather than sending raw audio to a server, which ensures user privacy and reduces bandwidth usage.

1. Component Selection:

The Brain (ESP32 SoC): We selected the ESP32 microcontroller because of its dual-core architecture. We dedicated Core 0 to handling the Wi-Fi stack and cloud communication, while Core 1 was reserved exclusively for the heavy math involved in real-time audio processing. This ensures that a poor Wi-Fi connection never interrupts the audio sampling process.

The Ear (INMP441): We chose the INMP441 MEMS microphone over cheaper analog alternatives (like the KY-037). The INMP441 is an omnidirectional sensor with a built-in 24-bit I2S interface. This design choice completely eliminates the "static" or "buzz" often found in analog sensors caused by power supply fluctuations.

2. Circuit Interfacing: The hardware architecture is streamlined for efficiency. The INMP441 is wired to the ESP32 via the I2S bus using three specific lines:

Serial Clock (SCK): Synchronizes the data transmission.

Word Select (WS): Tells the ESP32 whether the current data is for the Left or Right audio channel.

Serial Data (SD): Carries the actual audio bits.

C. Testing

To transition the system from a theoretical prototype to a deployable tool, we devised a three-stage testing protocol.

1. Calibration (The "Ground Truth" Test):

Before the device could be trusted, it had to be taught what "loud" means. We placed the NoizOff prototype in a sound-proofed environment alongside a Standard Industrial Sound Level Meter (SLM). We played white noise at varying volumes. By comparing the raw values of our prototype with the accurate reading on the SLM, we calculated the offset value ($C_{cal}$) needed to tune our software.

2. Environmental Stress Testing:
We then moved the device to three distinct real-world environments to test its versatility:

Zone A (Library): Tested the system's "noise floor" (sensitivity to quiet sounds).

Zone B (Busy Intersection): Tested the system's ability to handle chaotic, fluctuating noise without crashing.

Zone C (Construction Site): Tested the "Sustained Violation" logic, verifying that continuous drilling noise successfully triggered the email alerts.

3. Latency Analysis:
 Finally, we measured the "Time-to-  Alert." We used a stopwatch to measure   the delay between the moment the noise threshold was crossed and the moment the notification email arrived in the inbox. This metric is crucial because a delayed report is often useless in governance scenarios.

Technical Domain Observed Challenge Recommended Implementation Hint

Power Supply The ESP32 experiences high current spikes (up to 500mA) during Wi-Fi transmission, causing voltage dips that corrupt I2S audio data. Add Capacitors: Place a 10µF - 100µF electrolytic capacitor across the VIN and GND pins to stabilize voltage. Use a regulated 2A external power source rather than USB power during field tests.

Signal Integrity The I2S protocol operates at high frequencies. Wires longer than 15cm introduced significant impedance mismatch and "crackling" artifacts. Minimize Wire Length: Keep connections between the INMP441 and ESP32 under 10 cm. Use shielded twisted-pair cables for the SCK (Clock) and SD (Data) lines to reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI).

Acoustic Isolation Physical vibrations from the mounting surface or housing enclosure were often registered by the MEMS sensor as acoustic noise. Decoupling: Do not mount the sensor directly to the hard casing. Use high-density rubber grommets or foam to acoustically decouple the microphone.

Environmental Noise Outdoor wind gusts created low-frequency pressure waves (wind buffering) that mimicked high-decibel pollution. Wind Screening: Cover the microphone port with a thin layer of open-cell acoustic foam to filter out wind noise while allowing sound pressure to pass through.

Calibration Establishing a "Zero-Point" in an environment with ambient background noise resulted in permanently skewed decibel calculations. Quiet Baseline: Perform the initial software calibration in a sound-insulated room or during late-night hours to establish a true silence baseline before setting the gain.

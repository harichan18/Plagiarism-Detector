1. Introduction

A robot is built from three cooperating layers: sensing, processing and actuation. Sensors convert physical quantities such as distance, magnetic field, rotation, temperature and light into electrical signals. A microcontroller or single-board computer interprets these signals and drives motors or other actuators. Communication links, wired inside the robot and wireless to the outside world, carry data between sensors, controllers, other robots and cloud services.

This assignment explains six commonly used sensors (proximity, ultrasonic, Hall effect, gyroscope, temperature and light) in terms of working principle, interfacing method and applications. It then compares three wired serial protocols (RS232, I²C and SPI) and discusses five technologies used to connect robots to the Internet of Things: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth/BLE, Zigbee, LoRaWAN and MQTT.

2. Sensors for Robotic Systems

Sensors are classified as analog (output voltage varies continuously and is read by an ADC), digital (output is a logic level or a pulse width) or smart/bus sensors (an internal ADC and processor provide readings over I²C, SPI or UART). The interfacing method depends on which of these a sensor belongs to.

2.1 Proximity Sensors

Working principle

A proximity sensor detects the presence of an object without physical contact. The sensing physics differs by type:

Inductive: an internal oscillator drives a coil and produces a high-frequency magnetic field. When a metal target enters the field, eddy currents form in it and draw energy from the oscillator, so the oscillation amplitude drops. A trigger circuit detects this drop and switches the output. Inductive sensors detect metals only, with a typical range of 1 to 30 mm.

Capacitive: the sensing face forms one plate of a capacitor. An approaching object, metallic or non-metallic (plastic, liquid, wood), changes the dielectric and increases the capacitance, which shifts the oscillator and triggers the output. Range is typically 3 to 25 mm.

Infrared (optical reflective): an IR LED emits light and a photodiode or phototransistor measures the amount reflected from the object. Higher reflection means a closer or brighter object. Modules such as the FC-51 add a comparator and a potentiometer to set the detection threshold.

Magnetic reed or Hall based: detect a magnet attached to the target (see Section 2.3).

Interfacing method

Industrial inductive and capacitive sensors are usually three-wire devices (supply, ground, output) operating at 10 to 30 V DC with an NPN (sinking) or PNP (sourcing) transistor output. Because the output swings to the supply voltage, it must be conditioned before reaching a 3.3 V or 5 V microcontroller pin, using a voltage divider or an optocoupler for isolation. Hobby IR modules run directly from 3.3 or 5 V and provide a digital output (HIGH/LOW) that can be read on a GPIO pin, with an optional analog output that can be read by the ADC to estimate distance. For fast-moving targets the digital output can be attached to an external interrupt.

Applications

Obstacle detection and edge (cliff) detection in mobile robots

Line-following robots using reflective IR sensors

Part presence detection on conveyor belts and pick-and-place machines

Liquid-level detection with capacitive sensors

Limit switches and end-of-travel detection in robotic arms and CNC machines

2.2 Ultrasonic Sensors

Working principle

An ultrasonic sensor measures distance using time of flight (ToF) of sound. A piezoelectric transmitter emits a short burst of ultrasound (typically 40 kHz), the burst reflects from an obstacle, and a receiver detects the echo. If the round-trip time is t and the speed of sound is v (about 343 m/s at 20 °C), the distance is:

Distance = (v × t) / 2

The division by two accounts for the sound travelling to the object and back. The speed of sound changes with air temperature (approximately v = 331.3 + 0.606·T m/s, with T in °C), so accurate systems compensate using a temperature sensor. The popular HC-SR04 module measures roughly 2 cm to 400 cm with a beam angle of about 15 degrees.

Interfacing method

The HC-SR04 has four pins: VCC (5 V), Trig, Echo and GND.

The microcontroller sends a 10 µs HIGH pulse on Trig. The module transmits eight 40 kHz pulses and then raises Echo HIGH for a duration equal to the round-trip time.

The microcontroller measures the Echo pulse width, using a timer capture or a function such as Arduino pulseIn(), and converts it. With the pulse width in microseconds, distance in cm is approximately width / 58.

The Echo output is 5 V, so a 3.3 V controller (ESP32, Raspberry Pi) needs a voltage divider or level shifter on this line.

Waterproof versions (JSN-SR04T) and industrial versions with analog, UART or I²C output are also available.

Limitations

Soft materials (foam, cloth) absorb sound, angled surfaces reflect it away from the receiver, and a short minimum range (a blind zone) exists because the receiver is still ringing after transmission. Several ultrasonic sensors on one robot must be triggered in sequence to avoid cross-talk.

Applications

Obstacle avoidance and navigation in mobile robots and drones

Parking assistance and automotive proximity sensing

Water or fuel tank level measurement

Mapping a room by mounting the sensor on a servo (sonar scan)

Object counting and presence detection in industrial automation

2.3 Hall Effect Sensors

Working principle

When a current-carrying conductor or semiconductor plate is placed in a magnetic field perpendicular to the current, charge carriers are deflected sideways by the Lorentz force. This builds up a small transverse voltage across the plate, called the Hall voltage:

V_H = (I × B) / (n × q × t)

where I is the current through the plate, B the perpendicular magnetic flux density, n the carrier concentration, q the electron charge and t the plate thickness. The voltage is proportional to the magnetic field strength, so the sensor measures field magnitude and polarity. Internal amplifier, regulator and comparator circuits turn this tiny voltage into a usable output.

Hall sensors come in two main forms: linear (analog) sensors such as the SS49E and A1302, whose output voltage varies in proportion to field strength (centred at about half the supply voltage for zero field), and digital (switch or latch) sensors such as the A3144, which switch the output when the field crosses a threshold.

Interfacing method

Digital Hall switch: three pins (VCC, GND, OUT). The output is typically open-collector, so a pull-up resistor (about 10 kΩ) to the logic supply is required. Connect OUT to a GPIO or interrupt pin and count pulses.

Linear Hall sensor: connect the analog output to an ADC channel. Measure the voltage and convert to magnetic field using the sensitivity in the datasheet (in mV/gauss).

Current sensors (e.g. ACS712): the current to be measured flows through an internal conductor, the resulting magnetic field is sensed by the Hall element and an analog voltage proportional to current is produced, with galvanic isolation.

Speed measurement: a magnet is attached to a rotating wheel or shaft. Each pass produces a pulse, and RPM = (pulses counted / pulses per revolution) × (60 / time in seconds).

Applications

Wheel and motor speed sensing and encoders for odometry

Commutation of brushless DC (BLDC) motors, where three Hall sensors detect rotor position

Contactless current measurement in motor drivers and battery monitors

Position detection of robotic joints, grippers and linear actuators

Joystick and trigger position sensing, and door or lid open/close detection

2.4 Gyroscope Sensors

Working principle

A gyroscope measures angular velocity (rate of rotation) about one or more axes. Modern robots use MEMS (micro-electro-mechanical systems) gyroscopes built on a silicon chip. A tiny proof mass is driven to vibrate at a known frequency along one axis. When the device rotates, the Coriolis force acts on the moving mass perpendicular to both the vibration direction and the rotation axis:

F_c = −2 m (Ω × v)

where m is the proof mass, v its velocity and Ω the angular rate. This force displaces the mass in the sense direction, and the displacement is detected as a change in capacitance between fixed and movable electrode fingers. Signal conditioning converts the capacitance change to a digital value proportional to angular rate, usually in degrees per second (dps).

Orientation is obtained by integrating the angular rate over time. Integration accumulates bias and noise, so the angle slowly drifts. For this reason the gyroscope is combined with an accelerometer (which gives a gravity reference) and sometimes a magnetometer, using a complementary filter, Madgwick/Mahony filter or Kalman filter (sensor fusion). Such combined units are called IMUs (inertial measurement units).

Interfacing method

Common modules such as the MPU6050 (6-axis: 3-axis gyro plus 3-axis accelerometer) and the MPU9250 or BNO055 (9-axis) communicate over I²C (default MPU6050 address 0x68, or 0x69 when AD0 is high) and some also support SPI.

Wiring: VCC, GND, SDA and SCL to the controller's I²C pins, with pull-up resistors (usually present on the breakout board). An INT pin can signal data-ready to the controller.

Configuration: wake the device from sleep by writing to its power-management register, set the full-scale range (for example ±250, ±500, ±1000 or ±2000 dps) and the digital low-pass filter.

Reading: read the 16-bit raw registers for each axis, divide by the sensitivity scale factor (for example 131 LSB per dps at ±250 dps) and subtract a calibration offset measured while the sensor is still.

Run the fusion algorithm at a fixed sample rate (e.g. 100 to 500 Hz) to obtain roll, pitch and yaw.

Applications

Self-balancing two-wheeled robots (the tilt angle is fed to a PID controller)

Attitude and stability control of drones and quadcopters

Heading estimation and dead-reckoning navigation for mobile robots

Gesture control, camera gimbals and robotic arm end-effector orientation

Smartphones, game controllers and wearable motion tracking

2.5 Temperature Sensors

Working principle

Temperature sensors exploit physical properties that change predictably with temperature:

Thermistor (NTC/PTC): a semiconductor resistor whose resistance changes strongly with temperature. An NTC thermistor's resistance falls as temperature rises, following the Beta equation 1/T = 1/T₀ + (1/β) ln(R/R₀), or the more accurate Steinhart-Hart equation. Inexpensive and highly sensitive, but non-linear.

RTD (e.g. Pt100): a platinum element whose resistance increases almost linearly with temperature, R = R₀(1 + αΔT), with R₀ = 100 Ω at 0 °C. Very accurate and stable, but needs precise signal conditioning (often 3-wire or 4-wire connection).

Thermocouple (K-type etc.): two dissimilar metals joined at a junction produce a voltage proportional to the temperature difference between the hot junction and the cold (reference) junction, known as the Seebeck effect. It covers very high temperatures (up to over 1000 °C) but needs amplification and cold-junction compensation.

Semiconductor IC sensors: devices such as the LM35 (analog, 10 mV per °C), TMP36, DS18B20 (digital, 1-Wire) and LM75/BME280 (digital, I²C) use the temperature dependence of a silicon junction's base-emitter voltage and provide a calibrated output.

Interfacing method

Thermistor: place it in a voltage divider with a fixed resistor, read the divider voltage with the ADC, compute resistance and apply the Beta or Steinhart-Hart equation.

LM35/TMP36: connect the output directly to an ADC pin. For LM35, temperature in °C = (ADC voltage in mV) / 10. Averaging several samples reduces noise.

DS18B20: a 12-bit digital sensor (resolution 0.0625 °C, range −55 to +125 °C) using the single-wire 1-Wire bus. Data pin with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up to VCC; multiple sensors can share one pin because each has a unique 64-bit ID.

Thermocouple: use an interface IC such as the MAX6675 or MAX31855, which performs amplification, cold-junction compensation and digitisation and returns the temperature over SPI.

RTD: use a MAX31865 (SPI) or a precision current source and ADC.

Applications

Motor, battery and driver temperature monitoring and thermal protection in robots

Environmental sensing in agricultural and warehouse robots

Process control in industrial furnaces, 3D printers and heating systems

Temperature compensation for other sensors (e.g. speed of sound for ultrasonic sensors, gyroscope bias)

Fire and overheat detection in inspection robots

2.6 Light Sensors

Working principle

Light sensors convert incident light intensity into an electrical signal, using the photoelectric/photoconductive effect in semiconductors. Photons with sufficient energy create electron-hole pairs, which alter conductivity or generate current.

LDR (light dependent resistor, photoresistor): its resistance decreases as illumination increases, from megaohms in darkness to a few hundred ohms in bright light. Cheap but slow and imprecise.

Photodiode: a reverse-biased PN junction that generates a photocurrent proportional to light intensity. Fast and linear.

Phototransistor: a photodiode with built-in gain, giving a larger output current with somewhat slower response.

Digital ambient light ICs (BH1750, TSL2561, VEML7700): integrate a photodiode, ADC and logic, and report illuminance in lux directly.

Colour sensors (TCS3200, TCS34725): use red, green, blue and clear filtered photodiodes to identify colour.

Interfacing method

LDR: form a voltage divider with a fixed resistor (about 10 kΩ) and read the midpoint with an ADC channel; the voltage rises or falls with brightness depending on the divider arrangement. An LDR module adds a comparator for a digital dark/light output.

Photodiode: use a transimpedance amplifier (op-amp with feedback resistor) to convert the photocurrent to a voltage before the ADC.

BH1750: I²C device (address 0x23 or 0x5C) with a 1 to 65535 lux range. Send a power-on and measurement-mode command, wait for conversion, read two bytes and divide by 1.2 to get lux.

Line-follower arrays: pair an IR LED with a phototransistor and read the reflected level to distinguish dark and light surfaces.

Applications

Line-following and edge detection using reflected light

Light-seeking or light-avoiding (phototropic) robot behaviour, and solar-tracking robots

Automatic brightness control and day/night switching in smart lighting and displays

Colour sorting of objects on conveyors

Optical encoders, beam-break object counting and communication via IR/light

4. Wired Communication Protocols

Inside a robot, the controller exchanges data with sensors, displays, memory, motor drivers and other controllers over serial buses. Serial communication uses few wires, which reduces cost, weight and wiring complexity. The three protocols below are the most widely used.

4.1 RS232

RS232 (Recommended Standard 232, now TIA/EIA-232) is an asynchronous, point-to-point serial standard originally defined to connect a computer (DTE) to a modem (DCE). It uses no shared clock; both ends must be configured to the same baud rate (for example 9600 or 115200), number of data bits (usually 8), parity (none, even or odd) and stop bits (1 or 2).

Signal lines: a minimum connection needs TXD (transmit), RXD (receive) and GND, with optional handshaking lines such as RTS/CTS and DTR/DSR.

Frame format: the idle line is logic 1. Each byte is sent as a start bit (0), 5 to 8 data bits, least significant bit first, an optional parity bit and one or two stop bits (1).

Voltage levels: RS232 uses bipolar levels. A logic 1 (mark) is −3 V to −15 V and a logic 0 (space) is +3 V to +15 V. This differs from the 0/3.3 V or 0/5 V TTL levels of a microcontroller UART, so a level converter such as the MAX232 (or MAX3232 for 3.3 V) is required between them. Connecting RS232 directly to a microcontroller pin can damage it.

Characteristics: full duplex, one transmitter and one receiver per link, cable length of about 15 m at standard rates (longer at lower baud rates), no addressing. Longer or noisy links often use the differential RS485 standard, which supports multi-drop networks over up to about 1200 m.

Robotic uses: connecting GPS modules, GSM modems, industrial PLCs, motor drives and legacy instruments, and debugging a robot through a serial console.

4.2 I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit)

I²C was developed by Philips (now NXP). It is a synchronous, half-duplex, multi-master, multi-slave bus that uses only two wires: SDA (serial data) and SCL (serial clock). Both lines are open-drain and are pulled up to the supply voltage by external resistors (typically 2.2 kΩ to 10 kΩ, often 4.7 kΩ), which allows many devices to share the same lines safely.

Addressing: every slave has a 7-bit (or 10-bit) address, so up to about 112 devices can share one bus provided addresses do not clash. No chip-select lines are needed.

Transaction: the master generates a START condition (SDA falls while SCL is high), sends the 7-bit address plus a read/write bit, and the addressed slave replies with an ACK bit. Data bytes follow, each acknowledged by the receiver, and the master ends with a STOP condition (SDA rises while SCL is high).

Speeds: standard mode 100 kbit/s, fast mode 400 kbit/s, fast-mode plus 1 Mbit/s and high-speed mode 3.4 Mbit/s.

Features and limits: ACK-based error detection, clock stretching (a slave can hold SCL low to slow the master), arbitration between multiple masters. Bus capacitance (about 400 pF maximum) limits cable length to short on-board or inter-board distances.

Robotic uses: IMUs (MPU6050), real-time clocks, OLED displays, EEPROMs, ADC/DAC chips, PWM servo drivers (PCA9685), light and temperature sensors.

4.3 SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface)

SPI, introduced by Motorola, is a synchronous, full-duplex, master-slave bus designed for high speed. It uses four signals: SCLK (clock from master), MOSI (master out, slave in), MISO (master in, slave out) and CS/SS (chip select, active low, one per slave).

Operation: the master pulls a slave's CS low and generates clock pulses. On every clock cycle one bit is shifted out on MOSI while one bit is shifted in on MISO, so data is exchanged in both directions simultaneously through shift registers.

Modes: four modes (0 to 3) defined by clock polarity (CPOL) and clock phase (CPHA) decide when data is sampled and changed. Master and slave must use the same mode.

Speed: typically 1 to 50 MHz or more, much faster than I²C or RS232, as push-pull drivers give sharp edges and there is no addressing overhead.

Limits: needs one extra CS line per slave (or a daisy-chain arrangement), no built-in acknowledgement or error detection, no formal addressing, and short distances.

Robotic uses: SD cards, high-speed ADCs, TFT displays, flash memory, thermocouple interface chips (MAX31855), IMUs at high sampling rates, RF modules (nRF24L01, LoRa transceivers) and CAN controllers.

5. Comparison of RS232, I²C and SPI

Choosing between them. Use I²C when many low- or medium-speed sensors must share two pins. Use SPI when throughput matters, for example reading an SD card, driving a display or sampling a fast ADC. Use RS232 (or RS485 for longer, multi-drop links) when connecting to external equipment, computers or industrial devices that expose a serial port.

6. Wireless and IoT Communication for Robots

A robot becomes part of the Internet of Things (IoT) when it can send telemetry (battery, position, sensor data) to remote systems, receive commands and take part in multi-robot coordination. Choice of technology depends on range, data rate, power consumption, network size, cost and reliability. Wi-Fi, Bluetooth/BLE, Zigbee and LoRaWAN are wireless communication technologies (physical and link layers), while MQTT is an application-layer messaging protocol that runs over an IP network such as Wi-Fi or cellular.

6.1 Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11)

Wi-Fi is a wireless local area network (WLAN) technology operating in the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands (and 6 GHz in Wi-Fi 6E/7), with standards such as 802.11b/g/n/ac/ax. A robot connects to an access point as a station and obtains an IP address, which gives it direct access to the Internet and the cloud using standard TCP/IP, HTTP, WebSocket and MQTT.

Data rate: from tens of Mbit/s (802.11n) to hundreds of Mbit/s or more on newer standards.

Range: roughly 30 to 100 m indoors per access point, extendable with multiple access points or mesh.

Power: relatively high, with transmit currents of the order of 100 to 250 mA on typical modules, so it suits mains-powered or large-battery robots.

Modules: ESP8266 and ESP32 (a low-cost microcontroller with built-in Wi-Fi and Bluetooth), Raspberry Pi.

Robotic applications: live video streaming from inspection or telepresence robots, remote control from phones and web dashboards, ROS (Robot Operating System) communication between robots and a base computer, over-the-air (OTA) firmware updates, and cloud data logging.

Drawbacks: high power consumption, congestion in the 2.4 GHz band, and dependence on existing infrastructure.

6.2 Bluetooth and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)

Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology in the 2.4 GHz ISM band using frequency hopping. Classic Bluetooth (BR/EDR) provides a continuous stream at up to a few Mbit/s, using the Serial Port Profile (SPP) to emulate a serial cable. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), introduced in Bluetooth 4.0, is designed for small, infrequent data packets at very low power, allowing coin-cell operation for months or years.

Architecture: BLE devices use the Generic Attribute Profile (GATT): a server (the robot or sensor) exposes services and characteristics, and a client (phone or gateway) reads, writes or subscribes to notifications. Bluetooth 5 adds 2 Mbit/s mode, longer range (coded PHY) and larger advertising capacity; Bluetooth Mesh supports many-to-many networks.

Range and rate: about 10 m typical for classic and BLE, up to 100 m or more in good conditions with Bluetooth 5 long-range mode; BLE application throughput of the order of 100 kbit/s to 1.4 Mbit/s.

Modules: HC-05/HC-06 (classic serial), HM-10 (BLE), ESP32, nRF52 series.

Robotic applications: smartphone remote control of hobby robots and rovers, configuration and calibration of a robot over a mobile app, wearable sensors and tags, proximity-based pairing, and short-range communication between a robot and accessories.

Drawbacks: short range, small network size (without mesh) and limited bandwidth compared with Wi-Fi.

6.3 Zigbee

Zigbee is a low-power wireless mesh networking standard built on the IEEE 802.15.4 physical and MAC layers, with network and application layers defined by the Zigbee Alliance (now the Connectivity Standards Alliance). It mainly operates at 2.4 GHz (250 kbit/s), with 868 MHz and 915 MHz options in some regions.

Device roles: the coordinator forms and manages the network, routers extend the range by relaying messages, and end devices are low-power nodes that sleep most of the time.

Topologies: star, tree and mesh. In a mesh network messages hop through intermediate nodes and the network can self-heal if a node fails.

Range and rate: about 10 to 100 m per hop, extended across the mesh; data rate up to 250 kbit/s, which is enough for sensor readings and control commands but not for video.

Power and security: very low power, with battery life of months to years, and AES-128 encryption at the network and application level.

Modules: XBee (Digi), CC2530/CC2652 based modules.

Robotic applications: coordination of swarms or fleets of small robots, factory and warehouse sensor networks, smart-home automation and building control integration, and relaying telemetry across a large site where individual links are short.

Drawbacks: low data rate, not directly compatible with Wi-Fi (needs a gateway), and a more complex setup than Wi-Fi or BLE.

6.4 LoRaWAN

LoRa is a long-range, low-power physical layer using chirp spread spectrum modulation in licence-free sub-GHz bands (for example 865 to 867 MHz in India, 868 MHz in Europe and 915 MHz in North America). LoRaWAN is the open MAC-layer protocol on top of LoRa that defines how devices connect to the network and how data is secured, managed and routed.

Architecture: a star-of-stars topology. End devices send data to nearby gateways, which forward it over IP to a network server. The network server removes duplicates, handles security, and passes data to application servers (for example The Things Network or ChirpStack).

Range and rate: about 2 to 5 km in urban areas and 15 km or more in open rural areas. Data rates are low, roughly 0.3 to 50 kbit/s depending on the spreading factor (SF7 to SF12). Payloads are small (tens to a few hundred bytes), and duty-cycle limits restrict how often devices may transmit.

Device classes: Class A (uplink followed by two short receive windows; lowest power), Class B (scheduled receive windows) and Class C (continuously listening; highest power).

Security: AES-128 encryption with separate network and application session keys.

Robotic applications: agricultural robots and field sensors, tracking of robots, vehicles and assets over wide areas, remote environmental monitoring, and sending periodic status or alarm messages from robots operating beyond Wi-Fi coverage.

Drawbacks: unsuitable for real-time control, video or large data, and downlink capacity is limited.

6.5 MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport)

MQTT is a lightweight publish/subscribe messaging protocol standardised by OASIS and ISO/IEC 20922, designed for constrained devices and unreliable networks. It runs over TCP/IP (default port 1883, and 8883 with TLS encryption). It is not a radio technology; it carries messages over Wi-Fi, Ethernet or cellular links.

Architecture: clients never talk to each other directly. They connect to a central broker (such as Eclipse Mosquitto, HiveMQ or EMQX). A client publishes a message to a hierarchical topic, for example robot/arm1/temperature, and any client that has subscribed to that topic (or a wildcard such as robot/+/temperature or robot/#) receives it. This decouples senders from receivers in both time and space.

Quality of Service: QoS 0 (at most once, fire and forget), QoS 1 (at least once, acknowledged) and QoS 2 (exactly once, four-step handshake).

Useful features: retained messages (the broker stores the last value for new subscribers), Last Will and Testament (the broker publishes a pre-set message if a client disconnects unexpectedly, which signals that a robot has gone offline), persistent sessions and keep-alive pings.

Overhead: the fixed header is as small as 2 bytes, so MQTT suits low-bandwidth links and microcontrollers (libraries such as PubSubClient and Paho).

Security: TLS encryption, username and password or client-certificate authentication, and topic-level access control on the broker.

Robotic applications: streaming telemetry (battery level, position, sensor values) to a dashboard or database, sending commands to robots on topics such as robot/arm1/cmd, coordinating fleets through a shared broker, and integrating with cloud platforms (AWS IoT Core, Azure IoT Hub, Google Cloud IoT alternatives, ThingsBoard) and tools like Node-RED.

Drawbacks: the broker is a central point of failure unless clustered, it depends on TCP and so is unsuitable for hard real-time control loops, and standard MQTT has no built-in payload format (JSON is commonly used).

7. Comparison of IoT Technologies and Selection Guide

Selection guidance

High-bandwidth data (camera, ROS, OTA updates): choose Wi-Fi.

Control from a phone or tight battery budget, short range: choose BLE.

Many low-power nodes needing multi-hop coverage across a site: choose Zigbee.

Kilometre-scale coverage with tiny, infrequent messages: choose LoRaWAN.

Reliable messaging between robots, gateways and cloud services: use MQTT on top of Wi-Fi or cellular (or on top of Ethernet via a gateway for Zigbee and LoRaWAN data).

In practice these technologies are combined. For example, Zigbee or LoRaWAN nodes send data to a gateway, the gateway converts it to MQTT messages and publishes them to a cloud broker over Wi-Fi or Ethernet, and a dashboard subscribes to the same topics.

8. Integrated Robotic IoT Architecture (Example)

Consider an autonomous warehouse inspection robot to see how the pieces fit together:

Sensing layer: ultrasonic and IR proximity sensors for obstacle avoidance, a gyroscope/IMU for heading and balance, Hall sensors on the wheels for odometry, temperature sensors on motors and battery, and a light sensor for line following and lighting-aware camera exposure.

Internal buses: the IMU, light sensor and OLED display share an I²C bus; an SD card logger and a thermocouple interface use SPI; a GPS module is connected over a UART/RS232-style serial link; Hall sensors and proximity switches use GPIO interrupts; analog temperature and light sensors use the ADC.

Processing layer: an ESP32 or Raspberry Pi reads the sensors, fuses IMU and odometry data, runs the obstacle-avoidance and PID motor control loops and decides on actions.

Connectivity layer: Wi-Fi links the robot to the warehouse network for video and OTA updates; BLE is used by technicians to configure it from a phone; MQTT publishes telemetry and receives commands; LoRaWAN or Zigbee can carry low-rate alarms from remote or outdoor areas where Wi-Fi is not available.

Cloud and application layer: an MQTT broker feeds a database and a dashboard that shows robot position, temperatures and alerts, and allows operators to send mission commands.

9. Conclusion

Sensors give a robot awareness of its surroundings and of its own state. Proximity and ultrasonic sensors supply distance and obstacle information, Hall sensors provide contactless speed, position and current measurement, gyroscopes supply rotation rates for stability and navigation, temperature sensors protect hardware and support process control, and light sensors enable line following, ambient sensing and colour recognition. Their outputs are read through GPIO, ADC, pulse timing or serial buses depending on the sensor type.

Within the robot, RS232, I²C and SPI each fill a role: RS232 for external and legacy devices, I²C for many slow peripherals on two wires, and SPI for fast peripherals. For connection to the outside world, Wi-Fi offers high bandwidth, Bluetooth/BLE offers convenient low-power short-range links, Zigbee offers low-power meshes, LoRaWAN offers long-range low-rate links, and MQTT offers an efficient publish/subscribe messaging layer that ties the devices to cloud applications. Good robotic IoT design chooses each sensor, bus and network by matching its capabilities to the application's requirements for accuracy, speed, range, power and cost.
